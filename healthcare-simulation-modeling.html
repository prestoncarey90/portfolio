Abstract

Section Lead: Aachal Ghimire

This project looks at the problems in today’s health insurance system and how simulation modeling was, is, and could be used. Many patients struggle with losing coverage when they lose their jobs, not being able to afford preventive care, or dealing with confusing insurance rules. Health care providers also face challenges with billing, delayed payments, and limits on what care they can give. While some solutions already exist—like Medicaid expansion or using data to improve decision-making—they are limited by decision makers who are usually not actively engaged in the systems of health care. This review explores how simulation tools can help test new ideas, predict outcomes, and give patients clearer information about their options. It also highlights areas where we still need more research, especially in using machine learning in fair and understandable ways. Overall, the goal is to show how smarter tools like simulations can lead to a more fair, flexible, and helpful insurance system for everyone.

1. Introduction

Section Lead: Preston Leslie

The subject of healthcare is a deep well to draw from. It is a complex, interwoven system of medical knowledge, credentials, and international, state, and local laws. Healthcare is not just about the care of peoples’ bodies—it is a sign of humanity, a political statement, and a reflection of our relationship with the natural world. Drawing from this multifaceted background, our research focuses on the insuring of care, questioning how companies and governments that back such programs operate.

What systems do they rely upon? What does a good system look like? And specifically, what do these look like within the United States? In the United States, insurance is a critical safeguard against financial hardship. Without proper health coverage, many people are forced to accept the risk of medical emergencies rather than spend their hard-earned income on care. Many individuals suffer physically and mentally, and some even lose their lives while trying to protect their families from financial collapse.

1.1 Project Background

Section Lead: Preston Leslie

We evaluate various healthcare and health insurance simulation models, analytical tools, and systems theory discussions to assess suitable coverage for patients, discuss the impact on provider care, and examine how insurance companies use these methodologies to improve services. We approach this topic as both patients and healthcare workers experienced in navigating insurance workflows, brought together by a shared curiosity about system mechanics and cross-cultural perspectives (contrasting American-born and international healthcare structures).

1.2 What is Insurance?

Section Lead: Aachal Ghimire

“In simple terms, Insurance is an arrangement by which a company or government agency provides a guarantee of compensation for specified loss, damage, illness, or death in return for payment of a premium.” — Hashemi et al. (2019)

While health insurance might be assumed to handle all issues of the body and mind, in America it often covers only “extremely specific parts of the physical condition of the body” (Gilbert, 2022, 2:52). This standard definition excludes mental/behavioral health, as well as dental and vision care. For this review, we draw upon literature across dental, vision, behavioral, and medical domains, using “health insurance” and “healthcare insurance” as overarching terms.

Understanding health insurance is essential for making informed decisions, especially when trying to avoid unexpected medical bills:

  • Deductible: The out-of-pocket amount you must pay before insurance starts covering services.
  • Copay: A fixed fee (e.g., $25 per visit) paid even if your deductible is not yet met.
  • Coinsurance: The percentage split (e.g., 80/20) you pay after meeting your deductible.
  • Out-of-Pocket Maximum: The total cap on what you pay in a year for covered services.
  • Metal Tiers: Plan groupings (Bronze, Silver, Gold, Platinum) balancing monthly premiums against out-of-pocket costs during care.
  • Plan Networks: Structures like HMOs (requiring Primary Care Provider referrals) and PPOs (offering out-of-network flexibility at higher costs).

2. Simulation Modeling in Healthcare Insurance: A Literature Review

Section Lead: Preston Leslie

Our search process utilized digital libraries including Google Scholar and the Baruch College Research Library, alongside primary source discussions with insurance actuaries, healthcare workers, and clinical providers. Search terms ranged from broad concepts (“healthcare simulation modelling”) to specific techniques (“insurance discrete event simulation”). Publications from 2015–2025 were prioritized, complemented by foundational texts recommended by industry professionals.

3. Challenges and Trends

3.1 For Health Care Providers

3.1.1 Silos of Care

Section Lead: Preston Leslie

“Silos of Care” describe institutional departments with first-degree dependencies that fail to communicate vital updates to internal and external workflows. Decision-makers frequently lack healthcare delivery backgrounds, and authority figures are rarely engaged during system updates. Furthermore, simulation models frequently suffer from a lack of portability:

“Simulations at one location never appear ‘portable’ to another site. Instead, each location must create its own simulation, meaning that the whole modeling effort must be replicated.” — Roberts (2011, p. 1413)

Freebairn et al. (2016) elaborate on the disconnect between evidence and policy planning:

“Descriptive evidence and analytical studies are used to describe issues and inform priorities; however, evidence on the implementation and impact of interventions is less commonly used to inform program planning decisions and strategic actions… Policymakers require synthesised and localised data that contrasts and prioritises policy options, demonstrates effectiveness of interventions, demonstrates the need for a policy response, demonstrates cost effectiveness of actions, reflects the level of public support for a particular issue and personalises the problem.” (p. 2)

These dynamics are particularly critical across inpatient care facilities, emergency rooms, and referral channels—systems that exist outside primary care providers yet remain tied to insurance rules.

3.1.2 Resources Spent

Section Lead: Preston Leslie

Healthcare institutions expend immense administrative power interacting with insurance systems. Dedicated departments manage credentialing, referrals, medication prior authorizations, insurance navigation, and billing audit training. Prior authorizations for medications alone require non-clinical administrative staff to submit medical necessity documentation, taking 1 to 3+ business days and delaying necessary patient care.

3.1.3 How Patients are Treated

Section Lead: Preston Leslie

Insurance reimbursement schedules dictate provider behavior. To remain viable, practices must either increase patient volume (reducing visit length and overall quality) or reduce volume (accepting revenue loss or undertaking arduous contract renegotiations with insurers) (Dr. Glaucomflecken, 2021; Roberts, 2011).

3.2 For Patients

3.2.1 Cost

Section Lead: Aachal Ghimire

Evaluating plan costs remains an unguided financial gamble. High-deductible, low-premium plans risk severe out-of-pocket costs during emergencies, whereas low-deductible plans impose high monthly premiums ($1,000–$2,000+/month). Uncovered emergency expenses (such as non-covered ambulance transports) create long-term financial burdens that discourage patients from seeking timely care in the future (Choi et al., 2020).

3.2.2 Claim Delays & Denials

Section Lead: Preston Leslie

When insurance companies reject treatment coverage, patients face mandatory step-therapy regimens (trying alternative drugs first) or delays while non-practicing insurance reviewers evaluate peer-to-peer appeals against the clinical judgments of treating doctors.

3.3 For Insurance Companies

3.3.1 Rising Medical Costs and Premium Deficiencies

Section Lead: Aachal Ghimire

Direct communication with insurance actuaries highlights the pressure of rising medical claims spending. Between 2020 and 2021, medical claims spending rose by 9.4% while premiums increased by only 3.9%, pushing average Medical Loss Ratios (MLR) up to 91.8% (AJMC, 2022).

3.3.2 Technological Integration

Section Lead: Aachal Ghimire

Legacy IT systems hinder automation and AI integration. Rapid post-pandemic digitization led to fragmented data platforms, cybersecurity risks, and automated claim denial errors that demonstrate the ongoing necessity of human oversight (Financial Times, 2023; Solartis, 2023).

4. Solutions

Section Lead: Preston Leslie

Modern healthcare simulation has shifted from Discrete Event Simulation (DES) toward hybrid Agent-Based Models (ABM) and System Dynamics (SD):

“Dynamic simulation models allow for rapid integration and use of new evidence for policy analysis, make trade-offs of policy options explicit, and act as a vehicle for advancing controversial, contested and value-laden debates… Simulation modelling can test the potential impact of programs and policies in the ‘safety’ of a virtual environment before they are implemented.” — Freebairn et al. (2016, p. 3)

Recent frameworks (Diaz-Navarro et al., 2024) offer standardized simulation best practices to combat Silos of Care across clinical, administrative, and educational workflows. Additionally, insurers deploy Bayesian multinomial latent variable modeling to detect fraud, waste, and abuse by reducing high-dimensional behavioral variables into stable predictive covariates (Bayerstadler et al., 2016).

5. Further Research Needed

5.1 From Literature

Section Lead: Aachal Ghimire

  • Comorbidities: Integrating multi-condition health trajectories into insurance risk models rather than evaluating conditions in isolation.
  • Employment Decoupling: Designing alternative insurance models independent of employment to prevent loss of coverage during economic downturns (Choi et al., 2020).
  • Actuarial Machine Learning: Modernizing traditional actuarial frameworks (like CMS Actuarial Value Calculators) using machine learning while maintaining regulatory transparency, fairness, and bias prevention.

5.2 What We’d Like to See

Section Lead: Aachal Ghimire

Comparative studies contrasting corporate risk models against patient-centered preventive wellness strategies. Developing educational risk tools for patients can empower consumers to evaluate insurance plans aligned with long-term health needs.

6. Conclusion

Section Lead: Preston Leslie

While current insurance workflows offer partial solutions, they often lack flexibility, transparency, and impartiality. Simulation modeling—paired with machine learning and integrated workflows—provides a data-driven path to test policies safely, reduce administrative friction, and build equitable healthcare access.
